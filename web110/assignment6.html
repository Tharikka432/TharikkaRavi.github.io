Activity 1 — Internet Services Internet services allow us to access a huge amount of information such as text, graphics, sounds, and software over the internet. The diagram shows four different categories of internet services. The communication services include Electronic Mail, Telnet, Newsgroups, Internet Relay Chat, Mailing Lists, Internet Telephony, and Instant Messaging. Information retrieval services include File Transfer Protocol, Archie, Gopher, and VERONICA (Very Easy Rodent-Oriented Netwide Index to Computer Archives). Now let’s talk about the Transmission Control Protocol. TCP is a connection‑oriented protocol and offers end‑to‑end packet delivery. It acts as the backbone for connection. It has several key features such as stream delivery service, sending and receiving, bytes and segments, full‑duplex service, and reliability. The Internet Protocol is connectionless and unreliable by itself. To make it reliable, you have to layer it with other protocols. HTTP defines the mechanism for communication between the browser and the web server. Activity 2 — FTP & Intellectual Property (ExaVault Review) In 2026, FTP is no longer connected to anything in most browsers. The browser will either show a download dialog for a single file or simply not load the FTP link. There are three fast ways to access an FTP site today: A dedicated FTP client Your operating system’s file manager A cloud‑hosted file‑transfer platform Now let’s look at why FTP was dropped. It’s been around for more than 20 years, but there are reasons it had to go: no encryption, two ports, hostile firewalls, and usage cratered. None of this means FTP itself is dead. The protocol is still actively used for B2B file exchange, vendor data drops, and other tasks. To access the desktop version of an FTP client, you can use FileZilla, WinSCP, or Cyberduck. There are also ways to get the browser‑style experience back by transferring the file to your cloud‑hosted file‑transfer platform. Activity 3 — RIAA Privacy Review The RIAA works to protect intellectual property rights and creative freedom. They fight for state, federal, and global policies. Federal laws include the Copyright Act, the Digital Millennium Copyright Act, and the Music Modernization Act. These protect someone’s original art and set out the rights and responsibilities that govern the music business. State laws include Tennessee’s ELVIS Act, the Truth in Music Advertising Act, and the True Origin of Digital Goods Act. States often engage in issues that affect the music ecosystem, including consumer protection, contracts, rights of publicity, privacy, and torts. International policies include US free‑trade agreements such as the US‑Mexico‑Canada Agreement and other bilateral trade deals, the WIPO Berne Convention, the WIPO Copyright Treaty, the WIPO Performances and Phonograms Treaty, and the WTO TRIPS Agreement.
